### 7.1 Skills

**Why it is first:** At a career fair the recruiter is hunting **specific terms** from the req. If those terms are buried, they will miss them even when the candidate has the exposure.

**Placement:** Skills **at the top** — “handy and neat and serves as a summary of my strengths.” Agrees with TIH’s experienced-hire order; disagrees with UNC student samples that often put skills last.

**Preferred format**
- **2–3 categories**
- Related terms/tools as a **comma-separated** list after the category label
- Easy for ATS to parse; easy to add/remove skills as they become relevant

**Do not** write “beginner,” “proficent” (her spelling), or “advanced.” Those words mean different things to different people. Prove skill level in **work, projects, or education** bullets instead.

**If a JD requires a tool you lack:** spend time on a project or work experience that uses that skill, the tool, or something **transferable** — then list it. (Do not invent; go get the exposure.)

### 7.2 Work experience — bullet framework

Without a framework this section is “the wild wild west.” Every bullet should answer:

1. **what I did** — the accomplishment
2. **how I did it** — skills and technologies used

**Repeatable formula (verbatim order)**
1. **Strong verb** (implemented, accelerated, reduced, launched, …)
2. **Accomplishment** (outcome, proudest moments, **numbers/percentages**)
3. **By using X, Y, Z skills and technologies**

She says this same framing **carries into interviews** (stories stay consistent with the page).

**Density**
- Each bullet **≤ 3 lines**
- **2–4 bullets per role**, scaled by tenure and how recent the job is
- Using only **part** of the framework is OK while you work up to it

**Header pattern in her strongest example:** Title + location/remote, company, dates, then a **scope sentence** (not a bullet), then accomplishment bullets.

### 7.4 Projects and other work

Chicken-and-egg: no experience → create a section for courses, training, projects, competitions, events — anything that is **not** employment.

**Preferred project block**
- Project **title**
- **One-sentence** description
- **2–3 bullets** of specific contributions that **include technologies**

**Alternative:** a **second experience section** that mimics Work Experience, so technical community/side work is not drowned by day jobs.

**If the page is full:** put extras on **LinkedIn** instead of crowding the resume.

### 7.5 Other tips on this page

- Remember **space, formatting, consistency, and order**.
- **Narrow margins** if you need room on a **single-page** resume (agrees with TIH 0.5").
- Everything on the page must **serve the job search**. Cut items that mean nothing to you or the reader. Best resumes are “intentionally thoughtful, creative, and practical.”
- Steal **impact language** from [O*NET](https://www.onetonline.org/) job profiles; if you cannot do that, **mimic the job description**. “If you can’t run, walk. If you can’t walk, crawl.”
- Keep applying, even **multiple positions daily** (volume across the market). **Tension with TIH:** do not spray many unrelated roles at **one** company — recruiters see the full ATS history.

**Closing:** people quit searches because the resume does not explain *why* their skills fit. Everyone deserves a resume that does.

### 7.6 Related — A Tech Resume Checklist (same author, Jun 15, 2022)

- **URL:** [https://tiffanyjachja.medium.com/a-tech-resume-checklist-ec91bbd8d0c5](https://tiffanyjachja.medium.com/a-tech-resume-checklist-ec91bbd8d0c5)
- **Not linked** from the 2024 Elements post; it is her other dedicated resume article (hiring-manager review of **240+** engineering resumes in 3 months; Twitch resume reviews at [twitch.tv/tifftiffanny](https://www.twitch.tv/tifftiffanny), historically Wed 10pm ET).

Eight-item checklist:
1. Summary quickly/clearly states **who you are, what you’ve done, and what you’re interested in** (Elements 2024 barely discusses summary; TIH/FTL do).
2. Experiences and projects: skills used to accomplish **X with Y tool(s)**.
3. Omit generic/subjective statements; show **impact**.
4. **Past-tense action verbs**.
5. Work experience **reverse chronological**.
6. Skills section sorted by **most relevant** (to the role).
7. Clear **sections** and **consistent formatting**.
8. **Hyperlink** public projects and professional socials.

Definition she uses: a resume summarizes why you are uniquely ready for **this** employer’s opportunity. Accurate, concise, easy to read, and it should make **you** excited to tell the story in an interview.
